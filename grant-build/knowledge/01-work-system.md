## 1. The Core System (Foundational Architecture)

**What a "system" is.** A fixed set of parts done the same way every time to produce a result. The reason most people can't complete tasks isn't discipline — it's that they work at random times in random ways, and random inputs give random outputs. A repeatable framework removes the randomness. —Grant

**The 40 / 40 / 20 split.** Completing tasks = **40% environment prep + 40% work prep + 20% daily deadline decision.** These are not equal-weight steps done for their own sake; they are the three levers that make execution automatic. Run all three and the probability the task gets done is ~99%. —Grant

**The task war is won before the task begins.** If the preparation is completed, completion of the task is *inevitable* — you don't have to force it. Grant claims he can look at anyone's preparation and predict whether they'll work that day, because he knows the three things to look for. —Grant

**Accuracy of the correct task = 80% of the battle.** The tasks themselves aren't actually hard once prepped. The entire game is *picking the right one*. Most people who manage to work at all still do the wrong things, which move the ball zero. Do the right tasks and you make more progress in a 2–3 month window than in all prior years combined. —Grant

**What the system is NOT: "superspeed working."** Being productive is not going a million miles an hour. Rushing kills clarity, so nothing actually gets done — it only *feels* fast. The work happens at normal (even slightly slower than you'd like) speed, "undertone with clarity at all moments," so you always know exactly what you're doing. Normal speed × the right task × daily repetition = enormous output over a year. —Grant

## 2. Environment Prep (40%)

Split into two halves: **psychological** (what's on your mind) and **physical** (where you place your body). Both are mandatory.

### Psychological — the Open-Loop Capture System

**Open loop.** Anything unfinished or unresolved that pulls your attention — the most in-your-face, pressing stuff your brain is trying to hold. Your brain burns energy trying to remember and manage these, which drains focus. —Grant

**The stress reframe.** Stress is *not* "I have too much to do." Stress is "I have too much stuff I *think* I have to do that I don't have a clear game plan for." Convert the pile into a clear plan and organize it out of your head, and the stress drops — you feel clear, calm, and powerful. —Grant

**Open-loop trigger questions.** What's on my mind taking attention, even a little? Is something bothering me? Am I avoiding something that weighs on me? Is there something I need to tie up? Do I owe someone a response? Do I need to check in on someone/something? Is there something I'm worried I'll forget? Is there something broken just sitting there? —Grant

**The 3-step capture (how you *close* a loop, not just list it):**
1. Clarify exactly what the thing is.
2. Clarify exactly what is required of you — your part of the commitment.
3. Clarify the next immediate physical action (literally what you'd do).
Then **store it in a place you trust you'll revisit** — otherwise "remembering to check" becomes its own open loop. Optional add-on: assign each item a time / schedule it into the day. —Grant

### Physical — the Environment Checklist

Ranked, with the highest-leverage item first:

- **Certainty of no future interruption** *(most important)*. Not "it's quiet now" but "I *know* nothing and no one will interrupt me." Any background "someone might knock / call / need me" is a live open loop draining the whole session. —Grant
- **No messaging access to you.** Because humans are social creatures, an unanswered message sits at a "root chakra" level and your brain won't stop tracking who you owe. This includes messaging *on the work machine itself* — killing your phone but leaving Slack/iMessage on the laptop just relocates the loop. —Grant
- **No phone in the room.** Off, and physically in a different room. (Studying at school → leave the phone at home, go to the library.) —Grant
- **Clean.** An uncluttered space; mess is an open loop. —Grant
- **Silent, and will stay silent.** (Why Grant dislikes coffee shops for early-stage task-doers: noise plus an unfamiliarity/safety element that keeps you subtly alert.) —Grant
- **Calm / safe.** You're not tracking danger or discomfort in the space. —Grant
- **No negative people around** (physically present, not just messaging). —Grant

**Bonuses:**
- **2–3 locations, or 2–3 spots per location** (not mandatory; Grant has thrived with a single spot). —Grant
- **The prone / reclined position.** Physical comfort matters a lot; Grant works reclined ~90% of the time (laptop beside him on the desk, not on his lap) because sitting upright is energetically taxing for him. Story: at his old sales job he put his feet on the desk and worked leaned back — colleagues resented it until they saw he simply performed better that way. Experiment to find *your* position. —Grant
- **GIGA bonus — a separate work computer.** A machine with *nothing* on it: no messaging, no socials, nothing but work. Personal computers carry endless open loops (texts, tabs, feeds). Grant says if he were broke and starting over, he'd finance/save for a cheap $200–400 laptop purely for work — it's that important. —Grant

## 3. Work Prep (40%)

### The Three Questions

1. **What is the single most important thing to do here?** The one thing that moves the ball forward more than anything else.
2. **What needs to be done for me to call this a good session?** Specifically — which exact tasks finished make you say "nice, that was a good one."
3. **What prep can I do *now* to make this stupid-easy?** Get it "teed up" like a golf ball — set up and figure out in advance everything the task involves.
—Grant

### Core Metaphor — "Move the Remote"

To move a remote to the end of your hand, you push it there. Cause and effect. Most people, instead of pushing the remote, "build a website for the remote, make business cards for it, go to the gym to move it" — the remote never moves. **Guy A** journals, saunas, cold-plunges, meditates, works out, meal-preps, hops on a call — 700 activities, remote unmoved. **Guy B (you)** has one line on the list — "move remote forward" — and does it in ~3 seconds. Do the activity at the *nucleus* of what actually moves the ball. —Grant

### The Only Two Things in Business

In any business only two activities matter: **Product** ("What am I selling?") and **Sales** ("How am I selling this?"). Everything that isn't one of those is wasting time. The goal is *cash in hand*; cash requires someone to see the product and say yes. Business cards and websites don't help you decide what you sell or get a yes — so they're avoidance. —Grant

### "Prepping the Task Is Part of Doing the Task"

Grant's signature move. Prep bleeds into execution: because your brain doesn't register prep as "the hard thing," there's no resistance and no perceived risk, so you slide into it — and end up doing the whole task. A psychological hack: *prep your way into the work.* —Grant

*Worked examples:*
- **The course itself.** Day 1 task list = "all modules organized" (one item). Prep the night before = open the Google Doc, pull up his notes. Next day he just sat and organized. Then, while filming, hidden "side quests" surfaced (needed more research/old notes), turning a 2-day job into 3 — which is expected, not a failure.
- **Building a digital-product business.** "Figure out niche → figure out problems → decide product type." As he preps (Googling niches into a Google Doc), he hits "I can't find the problems until I pick the niche" — the prep *auto-prioritizes* for him. Picks a niche (e.g., "honey at home for moms," later "beekeeping PDFs"), uses AI to surface problems and product ideas, discovers there's a logical purchase/upsell sequence he didn't know existed.
- **Building a website.** Diving in reveals hidden dependencies (needs a payment processor, a Google email) he didn't know existed → old list aside, new list, same three questions.

### Two Types of Tasks + The Energy Rules

- **Ball-moving tasks** — actually push the project forward; require real energy ("glycogen"). Grant literally can't film videos without energy.
- **Prep tasks** — can usually be done on low energy.

**Rules:**
- If you're totally cooked, *just prep* whatever you can, so when clarity/energy returns you "strike like a sniper."
- If a task is done and you still have gas, prep the next task right then — and if energy remains, do it. Ride the energy: go-with-the-flow, but on top of a ruthless fixed process.

**Day tasks vs Night tasks.** Day tasks = heavy movers that push the project forward for real. Night tasks = admin, cleaning, organizing, small stuff — mapped separately so they don't sabotage the movers. —Grant

### The "Shitty First Draft" / Ring the Bell

Don't care about quality on the first pass — "just ring the bell." Move the ball; polish later. —Grant

### Sizing the Task List

The list should read: **"I can definitely do that, AND I'll feel good if it's done."** Usually 1–5 big movers; *often just one*. If it feels too big → cut it in half. If it feels confusing → prep more, or make the pending decision. —Grant

### The Two Causes of ~99% of Procrastination

1. Not thinking through / not prepping the task enough (too vague).
2. The task is too big in your mind (cut it in half and prep half).
—Grant

**On deadlines:** don't over-stress the deadline, because diving into real work reveals hidden tasks. When it does: set the old list aside, build the new list, run the same process. The loop repeats until you get what you want. —Grant

## 4. The Daily Deadline Decision (20%)

**You control the decision, not the outcome.** All you can control is deciding "this is what I'm doing today" and doing the work. `[CONTESTED — speaker frame]` Grant attributes the *result* to "the magical void" / to God: you eat the right calories and do the cardio, but how fast the fat burns "is up to God." Attributed to Grant's faith-inflected model of outcomes; noted as a worldview position, not a checkable fact. (Relevant: this aligns with Jacob's own faith frame — do the work, release the outcome.)

**The decision sets everything in motion.** Making a genuine decision is described as "the secret." —Grant

**The ADHD / anxiety reframe.** `[CONTESTED — speaker claim, presented as fact]` Grant claims "what people call ADHD" is really an *energy problem plus an indecision problem*, and that anxiety "feels hyper but is actually low energy in the body." This is Grant's own model and runs against clinical consensus — ADHD and anxiety are recognized diagnoses, not merely unprepped environments. Kept here as his framing, useful as a *productivity* lens (prep + energy + decision genuinely quiet a busy mind) but **not** a medical account and not a reason to dismiss a real condition. —Grant

**Deciding what you won't allow quiets the brain.** Put the phone in another room and *decide* it's no longer an option, and "your brain deletes it" and stops bothering you. "Making a true decision about what you will not allow is what quiets your brain." Pair a real decision with good energy → a quiet brain. Analogy: like a bully, if you seriously signal "don't mess with me," the impulse leaves you alone — energy reacting to energy. —Grant

**"I'm doing this," not "I hope I do this."** A real daily decision has power behind it and doesn't feel heavy. If the environment is bad you won't make a clean decision; if the work is unprepped you can't make a strong one — the three parts are interlocked. —Grant

**Soul-level commitment.** "Nothing is hard to get if you genuinely, at a soul level, commit to doing it." Almost no one has ever truly soul-committed to anything — that's most people's real bottleneck. Direct your energy at *clearing uncertainty* (task prep). Replace fear with fascination: "don't be afraid to wield the sword" — see how much your body can take. —Grant

## 5. It's All Won the Night Before

**The whole day is won the day before.** Take ~30 minutes the day/night before to organize the day, the tasks, and the environment, so you wake up and spend your best energy *attacking* instead of deciding. Day-of indecision and uncertainty will otherwise consume you. —Grant

**The three yes/no gate questions** (all three must be "yes"):
1. **Did I prioritize my energy?** (sleep, diet, health)
2. **Do I know *where* I'm doing the work today?**
3. **Do I know the *specific tasks*, and have I deemed them doable + "this will be a good day"?**
Three yeses → the work gets done, guaranteed. Grant has run this ~5 years; every single miss traces to one of these being "no." —Grant

**"Getting cute" gets punished.** Every time he strays — "I already know this, I'll skip prepping today" — he doesn't get work done. Straying from the system = not working. He calls the failure "embarrassing and disgusting." —Grant

**Travel example.** New city, no known workspace → look up the coffee shop or hotel room *the night before*, pick the time and place, so the arrow isn't spent on logistics. Can you sometimes save a day-of scramble? Yes ("I'll just go to Starbucks, boom") — but 99% of the time it's too much chaos and too many variables. —Grant

**The compounding ladder:** win the day → win the week → win the month → win the year → win your life. —Grant

**Today's meta-example.** He planned last night ("I'll be at this place, energy good, film these specific things"), showed up to a work pod, and filmed ~10 modules in 3–4 hours. Proof of the system on the very video teaching it. —Grant

## 6. Shoot One Big Arrow Daily

**You get one big arrow per day** — one ball-moving energy blast, fired once. The entire question: *are you shooting it at the right thing?* Progress isn't "do a million things / get more energy" — it's aiming the single arrow at the most important target for what you actually want. —Grant

**Aim vs. shoot.** Prep the night before = *aiming* the arrow. Waking up and doing the top task = *shooting* it. If you don't prep, you spend the arrow *on* the prep (figuring out what to do, gathering context) — and if you do that three days running, you spend three days "always in preparation, never actually doing anything." At some point you must prep with the system, then fire at the real thing day after day. —Grant

**Examples.** *Girlfriend:* to have conversations, prep the NJ→NY logistics (train, timing, what to bring) the night before, so the arrow is spent on conversations, not logistics. *Business:* is the arrow a revenue-generating activity — is it literally going to get the cash? (Recruiting/employees is a different, valid use of the arrow, but this is about the personal work only you can do now.) —Grant

**"Don't get cuter than the system."** Repeated as a law. Every time Grant thinks he's better than the system, he's punished — doesn't work, feels like "I suck," gets back on the wagon the next day. —Grant

**Hit the target daily and you "pass years in days"** — more progress in 7 days than in the prior 7 years. Most people spend huge energy going 100 directions and stay in the same spot. —Grant

**Grant at 22.** No system, ~1-in-10 hit rate — couldn't get himself to do anything. Drinking, weed, and heavy coffee wrecked his energy; he spent his one arrow "trying to figure out other stuff" and "on self-hatred." He also didn't know he needed to give himself *space to explore* to discover the right task. Now, with energy handled, he's ~10-in-10. —Grant

## 7. Design Your Day System (Life Design)

**Your dream is a specific day lived a certain way.** To reach that dream-day, you design many ordinary days that each take one step toward it. If the dream is 1,000 steps away, take one step a day — arrival is inevitable ("it could be no other way"). Level-one dreams are 6 months to 2–3 years out; a smaller version is reachable within a week. —Grant

**Start place + end place.** Map both realities. Reverse-engineer from the goal: *What do I want? Where/how do I get it? What information do I need?* — answering for both the destination side and the starting side. —Grant

**`[CONTESTED — speaker priority]` Financial first.** "Your financial is your foundation… the core of life design is how are we paying for this?" Grant's stated ordering principle; a values choice, not a fact.

**The current-day audit — the pivotal question.** Write out your literal current day, then ask: **"If I ran this exact day every day for 100 years, where would I end up?"** and **"Is this day sending me where I want to go?"** The doom-scrolling receptionist, run out 100 years, lands in the exact same spot. —Grant

**"Your current day and your task list need to have a baby."** Merge the goal-driven task list *into* the actual day so it gets done. Fill dead time with the tasks (the receptionist studies during downtime); if the job won't allow it, carve a fixed 1–2 hours after work. —Grant

**The Escape-Plan Schedule.** When you can't get yourself to work at home, *change the environment*: get off work → drive to the library (not home) → leave the phone in the car → grind 5:00–7:00. This is the literal move Grant used to get out. —Grant

**One goal per day + the scientific loop.** The extended Scottsdale example runs: find remote jobs → learn resume/interview → apply → observe results → diagnose the failure (no interviews = resume or application problem; interviews but no offer = interview problem) → put *that fix* into the next day → repeat until solved → job → living options → move. Each new problem becomes the next day's single goal. —Grant

**Energy tradeoff.** He wanted to gym *and* research after work but had no energy for both. Fix: do the most important thing (research/work) first while energy exists, lift after. The priority gets the energy. —Grant

## 8. Actually Implementing It (Idea → Soul)

**Lock it in.** Knowing the system as an *idea* isn't enough; specifics only reveal themselves by doing it. Take a valuable thing and "spam the living hell out of it" via repetition + action until it's embedded "into your soul." Two stories: sales trainees who learned the perfect objection-handle and forgot it within 7 days for lack of reps; a golf-swing fix that needs ~100 practice reps or it's gone. —Grant

**The protocol: run it daily for 7–14 days (up to a month) without missing.** Prep the night before → wake → attack → repeat, no misses, until it's automatic and portable. —Grant

**What a run looks like:** show up with good energy, knowing exactly what you're doing and when, zero open loops, environment prepped, and go all in — friction removed. Grant demonstrates portability by filming from a "ghetto NJ Airbnb" in a sketchy chair: the system travels because he did all three preps. —Grant

**The rest of the course = diagnostics.** Everything after the core system exists to show *why you can't run it* (energy, focus, unclear tasks) so you can fix it. The core point is simply to run the system, every day, over and over. —Grant

## Step-by-Step Procedure (the system, reconstructed as taught)

**The night before (aim the arrow — ~30 min):**
1. **Prioritize energy** — protect sleep, keep diet clean, don't sabotage tomorrow's tank tonight.
2. **Open-loop dump** — write down everything pulling attention; close each with the 3-step (what it is → your part → next physical action); park it in a trusted place.
3. **Pick the ONE big arrow** — the single most important, ball-moving task.
4. **Define "good session"** — the specific finished tasks that make tomorrow a win (1–5 items, often 1).
5. **Tee it up** — do the prep that makes it frictionless: gather assets, open docs, make the pending decisions, break anything too big in half.
6. **Decide WHERE** — the specific location you'll work.
7. **Run the 3-question gate** — energy prioritized? place known? tasks known + doable + "good day"? All yes.
8. **Make the decision** — "I'm doing this," not "I hope to."

**The day of (shoot the arrow):**
9. **Lock the physical environment** — phone off and in another room; messaging killed (including on the work machine); clean, silent, no interruptions guaranteed, comfortable position.
10. **Shoot** — do the most important task first, at normal speed, with clarity. Ring the bell; shitty first draft is fine.
11. **Ride energy** — cooked → prep the next tasks; gas left after finishing → prep and then do the next.
12. **Night/admin tasks** — handle the low-energy small stuff only after the mover.

**The loop:**
13. **Re-prep on discovery** — when diving in reveals hidden tasks, set the old list aside, build the new list, run the same process.
14. **Debrief** — note where energy actually went and what broke; feed it into tomorrow's prep.
15. **Lock-in** — run the full cycle daily for 7–14 days without missing until it's automatic.

## Synthesis (angled to your daily planning + PeakProductivity skill)

The entire course reduces to one sentence: **aim one arrow the night before, then shoot it at the single ball-mover — in a prepped, interruption-free environment, on real energy — every day, until it's automatic.** Everything else (environment prep, work prep, the decision, design-your-day, the diagnostics) is scaffolding that guarantees the arrow gets *aimed* in advance so your peak energy is spent *shooting*, not figuring out what to shoot at.

The core mechanism underneath every technique is **moving decision-load off the execution window.** Open-loop capture, night-before planning, teeing up, and "deciding what you won't allow" are all the same move: pre-make the decisions so the working brain faces zero uncertainty and can just act.

**Highest-leverage application for you specifically:** the shift from *morning dump-and-sort* to *night-before aim.* On a morning-planning pattern, the arrow gets spent on aiming — sorting lists, deciding, re-sorting — which is exactly the "three days always in preparation, never doing" failure Grant names. Move the sort to the night before, pre-commit the one arrow (for an agency, bias it toward Product/Sales — client delivery and revenue, the actual remote), and wake up with nothing to decide and only the shot to take. Energy routing and the calendar check sit on top: full tank → fire the arrow at the mover; cooked → prep and let admin be the night task; and the arrow only counts once it's dropped into a real, protected calendar block — the task list and the day "having a baby."
