## PART ONE — THE EXECUTION PHILOSOPHY (speed over perfection)

This is one connected doctrine spread across four modules (Nine Commandments, 48-Hour Rule, Contact Points & Perfectionism, and pieces of Figuring Out What To Do). Merged here.

### Contact Points
A **contact point** is any moment you expose your work or idea to reality and get real feedback: a user, a customer, a critique, actual data. It converts theory and internal assumption into tested fact. Without contact points you are guessing at what "good" looks like, and guessing in isolation leads to immobility. **True perfection only emerges after roughly 100 iterations** of feedback and improvement, so trying to perfect anything before the first contact point is structurally impossible. —Grant

### The 70% Rule
Don't wait for full certainty. **Reach about 70% directionally correct, then move** and get a contact point immediately. Feeling 100% certain is a mental construct, not a prerequisite for action. The goal is to acquire contact points *faster and in greater volume than anyone else*, described hyperbolically as 100x more than competitors. —Grant

### Massive Imperfect Action
Swift imperfect action plus continuous iteration beats procrastination-by-perfectionism every time. Perfectionism is a trap: the moment something feels "perfect" in your head, that's the proof it hasn't been tested in reality. Launch sloppy-but-functional, learn, iterate. —Grant

### The 48-Hour Rule
**Nothing should take longer than 48 hours to reach its minimum viable version.** The constraint forces prioritization, strips the non-essential, and produces something real to get feedback on. People chronically overestimate timelines (weeks for what's actually days or hours); the 48-hour frame exposes the simpler, faster path. Aligned with first-principles / timeline-compression thinking (the summary name-checks Musk). Worked examples: hiring a sales rep compressed from weeks to 48h via existing contacts; a dev feature estimate dropping from 3 weeks to 48h once reframed to essentials; a course "weeks" of work done in hours once broken down. —Grant

### The Nine Productivity Commandments
Born from managing a slow employee who took 7 months to deliver nothing. Blunt by design:
1. If 70% directionally correct, get the contact point *immediately* — find the person with the info within 48h.
2. Iterate the same way at every step: 70%, move, next contact point ASAP.
3. Aim for 70% certainty but get contact points *far faster* than competitors.
4. If a task takes 3–10 minutes and you have the time, **do it now**. Don't let small tasks pile.
5. Get the needed contact point within 2 hours if possible, 48 hours maximum.
6. View projects in **48-hour windows**; push each stage to complete inside one.
7. Constantly check whether your current hypothesis actually has a contact point supporting it, or whether it's wasted effort on an unvalidated "ghost."
8. **Moving fast is lighter than going slow** — speed reduces the mental weight that perfectionism piles on.
9. Identify the most important **70% of the project and focus only on that** ("the bullet"); the remaining 30% ("the blanket") comes later.
—Grant

**Supporting terms:**
- **Spawn the human** — when you need knowledge/feedback, find the specific person who has it, fast (2h ideal, 48h cap), instead of theorizing alone.
- **Bullet and blanket** — the bullet is the core essential that must ship; the blanket is the secondary polish. Bullet first, always.
- **Active management is unavoidable** — even competent hires need oversight and accountability; hiring is not delegation-and-forget. —Grant

## PART TWO — WHAT TO EVEN DO (the real work is deciding)

### Figuring Out What To Do Is The Work
The hardest, most valuable part of work is **not execution, it's deciding what to execute.** Prioritizing, strategizing, reverse-engineering the goal to find the highest-leverage activity. This is why CEOs are paid so much and why most people undervalue it: thinking is invisible and taxing, so the "masses" dismiss it as "not working" and confuse being busy with being productive. `[CONTESTED — speaker framing]` Grant frames this in status-loaded terms ("peasant paradigms," low-consciousness "masses"); the useful core underneath is simply that cognitive prioritization is real, high-value work. —Grant

### The 70/30 Split
**Figuring out what to do can be ~70% of productive effort; execution is ~30%,** especially on anything new. Spend the front of a new project understanding the problem and sequencing before touching physical execution. —Grant

> **`[NOTE: apparent tension, resolvable]`** This 70/30 (thinking-heavy) sits against the execution philosophy's "70% certainty then move." They reconcile: think hard to *choose the right target and sequence*, but don't think your way to false 100% certainty on *execution* — pick the target (thinking), then move at 70% and let contact points finish the job. Thinking is for direction; speed is for the doing.

### The Productivity Paradox
You must hold two opposing truths at once: sometimes impose strict timelines to force action, other times grant yourself open space to explore and learn without pressure. Too much forced urgency skips necessary understanding; too much unfocused exploration stalls. The skill is judging which mode the moment needs, and building your *own* rules/systems rather than waiting for an external rulebook. Self-governance is the differentiator. —Grant

### Overcoming Indecision / Going Deeper
- Indecision comes from overvaluing the "right" choice — **"decision greed,"** trying to optimize maximum gain before acting.
- **Imperfect forward movement beats stagnation** because it produces learning.
- The real lever is **competence through depth, not the initial choice.** Being able to make *any* reasonable path work outweighs picking the "perfect" path.
- **Ocean metaphor:** beginners are "2 inches in" and can't see that mastery requires going miles deep; they imagine a shallow, short involvement instead of a long journey.
- Drop the **hero's-journey myth** that every early decision must be grand and perfect.
- **Scaling proof:** $10k/month feels impossible early; after enough depth, a $100k day becomes easier than that first milestone was. Ease is a product of prior depth, not luck or the initial pick.
- Prescription: pick the most obvious or interesting option in front of you, commit, and make "go deeper" the goal. Clarity emerges from committed effort, not before it. —Grant

## PART THREE — THE AVOIDANCE STATES (fiddling, procrastination, boredom)

### Fiddling vs Doing
- **Fiddling** = busy, unfocused, low-impact motion that doesn't advance the core task; driven by indecision and weak prep. **Doing** = focused, decisive, high-impact execution.
- Fiddling is almost always **decisions not made beforehand.** Two flavors of the indecision: *choice uncertainty* (which task) and *design/product uncertainty* (what the thing should be).
- Cure = aggressive prep: know the single most important task before you sit, and what a good session looks like.
- **Mid-task decisions:** distinguish *thinking a decision through* (productive) from an *indecision loop* (fiddling). If a decision needs real bandwidth and isn't blocking, defer it as an open loop and keep moving. If it's essential and blocking, make it fast.
- **Hidden avoidance / emotional block:** sometimes you fiddle because you don't actually want the outcome. Grant's example: a product design that required managing employees, which he disliked, so he stalled; he redesigned it to remove the management need and velocity returned. Meta-awareness: if you're fiddling, ask whether you even want the result, and redesign if not.
- Ethos line: **"Prep like a savage, decide like a predator, attack the most important task with ruthless precision."** —Grant

### Curing Procrastination (99% = two causes)
Same two causes named in the work system, expanded:
1. **Not specific enough.** The brain avoids vague tasks because it can't estimate the energy required or the odds of success, so it conserves and avoids. "Build a website" is a threat; "research 3 template designs, pick a builder, watch one tutorial" is not.
2. **Task too big.** Cut it in half, or commit to "just do half." Naming the concrete sub-steps makes progress feel achievable and creates momentum.
- Prepare the specific, granular list **the night before** so starting requires no thinking.
- **Energy is necessary but not sufficient** — good sleep gives you the *potential*, but without specificity you still procrastinate.
- Use **AI to list what's needed** and **YouTube tutorials** to fast-track unfamiliar tasks so they stop being unknowns.
- **Align tasks with your strengths.** Grant switched from LinkedIn cold outreach (tedious for him) to cold calling (suited him) and resistance dropped.
- **Most "big" tasks are actually short** — often 10 minutes to a couple of hours. The barrier is mental, not temporal. —Grant

### Boredom Is a Blessing
- Boredom is **unavoidable even in work you love** — the repetitive, maintenance parts.
- It's a **signal of normal progress**, not a sign you're on the wrong path.
- The **"boredom wall"** is where most people quit; enduring it is the differentiator.
- Reframe as **privilege/gratitude** — boredom means safety and stability, not crisis.
- Swap the internal script: "I'm wasting time" → "I'm progressing"; "boredom is bad" → **"boredom is my friend."** —Grant

## PART FOUR — CONTEXT & CLARITY MANAGEMENT

### Context Switching / Task Batching
- **Context switching is one of the most energetically expensive things the brain does.** Each task type is a "mental file" or browser tab that must be opened, loaded, and closed; jumping between unrelated files drains energy and causes "task whiplash."
- **Batch similar tasks** into dedicated blocks or days: a writing day, a filming day, an admin block. Keeps one context loaded instead of thrashing.
- **Respect dependencies** — sequence batches logically (script before film).
- Put the **highest-cognitive work in your highest-energy window**; batch low-demand admin into low-energy periods.
- Structure the day into ~2 major periods (morning/afternoon), 3 at most.
- **Avoid fragmented microtasks** across many contexts; that scatter is pure waste.
- **Close loops fully** — finish one big chunk before moving on rather than nudging five projects an inch each. —Grant

### Clarity Wave Fatigue / The Clarity Tax
- **Clarity wave fatigue:** the drain and time-loss of re-orienting to an unfinished task after a break. The **clarity tax** is that re-orientation cost, and it **grows exponentially** the longer a task stays open across days or weeks.
- **The brain does not reliably retain task detail across breaks.** Assuming you'll remember where you were is a fallacy.
- **Fix: super-detailed notes at every breakpoint.** Capture four things: *where am I exactly, how am I thinking about it (current mental model), what's the next step, and any essential context.* Grant's evolution: from useless fragments ("John, bionic leg") to that structured format.
- **Sales-rep story:** he avoided notes, redid whole conversations, lost months. Investing 3–4 minutes post-call in structured notes saved hours and closed more deals. Reframe: **notes are future earnings**, not admin overhead.
- Returning to a task cold burns your best cognitive fuel (glycogen, focus) on *re-orientation*, leaving you cooked before the real work starts.
- Batching reduces the number of start-stop cycles and therefore the tax. —Grant

## PART FIVE — REST & RECOVERY

### Redefining Rest
- Two kinds: **biological rest** (sleep, physical recovery) and **psychological rest** (mental relief from stress and open loops). Modern exhaustion is **mostly mental, not physical.**
- **Bad exhaustion** = tired from *avoiding* what you should do; restless, guilty, doesn't produce real rest or good sleep. **Good exhaustion** = tired from productive work; enables deep rest and quality sleep.
- **Open loops block rest.** You can't truly relax while unfinished tasks and unresolved decisions occupy background bandwidth. Closing loops (writing them out, making the decision, getting the answer) *is itself* a form of rest.
- **Non-avoidance fun:** genuinely restful leisure is fun you engage in *without* guilt, which is only possible once you're not running from work. Fun-as-avoidance doesn't restore you.
- Prescription: work in cycles (e.g., 2–3 focused days, then planned fun), let yourself fully chill once the day's work is done as well as it can be, and don't linger in **limbo** (neither working nor resting), which is the worst state of all. Prep and a trusted task system are what earn the guilt-free rest. —Grant

### Falling Off The Wagon
- Falling off is **inevitable but largely preventable**, and recovery is a skill.
- **~99% of distractions are your own** environment or prep failure, not bad luck.
- When you slip, find the **"seed cause"** — the initial decision or condition that let the distraction in (phone within reach, noisy room, working at a low-energy time), not just the surface distraction.
- **Don't self-flagellate.** Accept it, analyze calmly, redesign the system so that specific failure can't recur.
- Most failure traces to **environment and energy scheduling** problems, i.e., design problems.
- The day is **won the night before**; recovery = doing tomorrow's environment prep, work prep, and sleep tonight so you wake knowing exactly what and where.
- **Story:** working 16-hour corporate-sales days, he couldn't produce at night. Tried an 11am–8pm shift (low morning energy, failed), settled on 6am–2:30pm, then went straight to a library after work for 4–5 distraction-free hours before eating. Structure plus environment redesign fixed it. —Grant

## Source Contradiction / Tension Flags

1. **"70% and move" (execution) vs "70% of the work is thinking" (figuring out what to do).** Resolved above: think to pick the *target and sequence*, then execute at 70% certainty. Direction is thinking; doing is speed.
2. **"Move fast, imperfect action" vs "go deeper / competence takes miles of depth."** Not actually opposed: speed is *per iteration*, depth is *cumulative over many iterations*. Fast reps are how you get deep. The enemy of both is stalling.
3. **48-hour urgency vs the productivity paradox's "grant yourself open space to explore."** Grant names this one himself: some moments need a forced clock, others need unpressured exploration; judgment picks the mode. Don't apply the 48-hour hammer to genuine open-ended learning, and don't hide in "exploration" to dodge a shippable task.

## Decision Rules (the actionable core, distilled)

- Task feels heavy → it's **too vague** (specify it) or **too big** (cut in half). Those two cover ~99% of procrastination.
- Stalling / moving things around → you're **fiddling**; a decision wasn't pre-made. Name the one most-important task and commit, or check if you even want the outcome.
- Waiting to be sure → you're at **decision greed / perfectionism**. Move at 70%, get a contact point.
- Estimating "weeks" → ask **"how could this be done in 48 hours?"** and do that version.
- Breaking off a task mid-stream → leave **structured notes** (where / thinking / next / context) before you stop.
- Many mixed small tasks → **batch by context**; don't thrash between mental files.
- Feeling bored → **signal of progress**, push the wall; don't read it as "wrong path."
- Tired but restless → **bad exhaustion** from avoidance; the fix is doing the thing, then guilt-free rest, not more limbo.
- Fell off → find the **seed cause** (usually environment/energy/scheduling), don't self-attack, redesign tomorrow tonight.
- New/ambiguous project → spend the front on **thinking (what/sequence)**, then switch to **speed** for execution.
